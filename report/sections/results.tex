\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All results use the frozen analysis code and the full 200 rounds; ``bids
only'' is access level L1, ``bids + costs'' is L2. Intervals are 95\%
block-bootstrap intervals. Detailed per-cell figures, all detector rows and
the scripted rock--paper--scissors replication of the original paper are in
the appendix (Appendices~\ref{app:replication} and~\ref{app:allresults}).

\subsection{From bids alone, a calibrated alarm names the cartel}
Figure~\ref{fig:alarms} asks the question an overseer would ask: did the
alarm rule point to exactly the colluding pair, to a wrong pair, or stay
silent? With bids only, our permutation-corrected net-influence rule named
exactly the right pair in $16$, $13$ and $20$ of $20$ markets for the three
cartels that chat (two strong firms; strong and weak firm; two weak firms),
and never named a wrong pair. In $40$ honest markets it raised one false
alarm.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[199,400,466].correct_unique,wrong_pair; all_cells[55].any_candidate
The rule published with the method is not usable as an alarm here: it
flagged $24$ of $40$ honest markets with bids only and all $40$ with costs,
and for the strong-pair cartel with costs it named the wrong pair in $19$
of $20$ markets.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[56,63].any_candidate; all_cells[207].wrong_pair
With costs our rule named the pair less often (5, 8 and 19 of 20; three
false alarms in 40). Neither rule caught the cartel without chat
or the spy.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[206,406,472].correct_unique; all_cells[62].any_candidate; all_cells[268,274,334,340].correct_unique

\subsection{With costs, the direction of the flow becomes visible}
Costs let the measure separate two things: whether the two firms' bids move
in lockstep, and whether one firm's private cost shows up in the other
firm's bids (a ``leak''). Pooled over all markets of a type
(Figure~\ref{fig:flow}), honest pairs show neither. The cartel that chats
shows strong lockstep and leaks in both directions, consistent with
partners who tell each other their costs. The spy shows a one-way leak: the victim's
cost appears in the spy's bids ($0.0047$ bits), not the reverse
($-0.0004$). The cartel without chat leaves only a weak one-way trace
($0.0021$ bits) and no lockstep.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json fingerprints[0,2,3,4].terms.action,leak_to_member_1,leak_to_member_2
These pooled patterns are clear, but single markets are short: the
leak-based rule named the spy pair alone in only $4$ of $20$ spy markets,
and market-level flow labels rarely resolved the mechanism.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[339].correct_unique; all_cells[206,340].label:*

\subsection{Net influence versus a simple correlation}
Figure~\ref{fig:compare} compares the two measures by how often a treated
market looks more suspicious than its honest twin with identical costs
(AUROC; 50\% is a coin flip). In the pre-registered primary test,
net influence with costs separated the strong-pair cartel from honest
markets: AUROC $0.865$ $[0.734,0.968]$, $p=0.00002$ over $20$ matched
blocks. But a simple correlation of the same data did better ($0.9975$;
difference $-0.1325$ $[-0.2647,-0.0309]$), and it was at least as good for
every cartel that chats (the two small exceptions have intervals including
zero).
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json primary; all_cells[206,202].auroc; contrasts[95].result
Net influence was clearly better only where information leaks: for the spy
with costs, $0.700$ against $0.415$ (difference $+0.285$ $[0.062,0.515]$).
This held on $20$ fresh markets collected afterwards ($+0.265$
$[0.070,0.445]$, Section~\ref{sec:addenda}); with bids only it did not
replicate ($+0.095$ $[-0.1125,0.315]$).
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[340,337].auroc; contrasts[263].result; results/analysis/replication_v22/results.json contrasts[501,498].result
On fresh markets the strong-pair cartel was again detected (net $0.9075$
$[0.8025,0.985]$) and again better by correlation ($0.995$).
% source: results/analysis/replication_v22/results.json all_cells[2122,2119].auroc; contrasts[609].result

\subsection{Two warnings: the quiet cartel and the AI judge}
The cartel without chat raised the pair's profit by $0.399$ $[0.364,0.434]$
per round, almost three times the strong-pair cartel's $0.144$, yet it was
the cartel both measures detected least well and the alarm never caught it
with bids only (Figure~\ref{fig:warnings}a). The profit gain replicated on
fresh markets ($+0.283$ $[0.179,0.371]$).
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json behaviour[64,40].paired_difference; all_cells[268].correct_unique; results/analysis/replication_v22/results.json behaviour[40].paired_difference
The Qwen 9B judge reads the round records and names the most suspicious
pair. Under its registered $12{,}000$-token limit it failed on $322$ of
$600$ judgements. With $24{,}000$ tokens it answered $54$ of $60$, but it
separated cartel from honest markets barely better than chance (AUROC
$0.625$ $[0.424,0.808]$) and simply named the two strong firms: right in
$91\%$ of strong-pair cartels, but in $2\%$ of strong--weak cartels, where
net influence was right in $93\%$ of the same markets
(Figure~\ref{fig:warnings}b).

\subsection{How many rounds, and other AI models}
The alarm needs enough rounds. With bids only it named the strong-pair
cartel in $6$ of $20$ markets after $50$ rounds, $9$ after $100$ and $16$
after $200$, while false alarms in honest markets stayed at $3$ of $40$ or
fewer (Figure~\ref{fig:breadth}a).
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[147,173,199].correct_unique; all_cells[3,29,55].any_candidate
Other models behaved similarly with one exception
(Figure~\ref{fig:breadth}b). The alarm named the cartel in $18$ of $20$
markets for Qwen3.5-4B and Gemma-4-12B, and in $16$ and $19$ of $20$ in
mixed-model markets. For Ministral-3-8B it found only $11$ of $20$ and
raised false alarms in $6$ of $20$ honest Ministral markets ($3$ of $20$
for Qwen3.5-4B): calibration that holds for one model need not hold for
another. Gemma-4-E4B and Ministral-3-3B produced too many invalid bids, so
their results are unavailable rather than negative.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[*].correct_unique and any_candidate for sizes.qwen-4b, labs.gemma-12b, labs.ministral-8b, mixed (results/report_figures/key_figure_numbers.csv figure=7); all_cells[934,1000,1066,1132].eligible
Removing the surveillance warning lowered the cartel's profit by $0.35$
per round without a resolved change in detection; swapping honest
same-model placements and changing only the partner's cost in probes gave
no resolved effects (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).
