\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All results use the frozen analysis code and the full 200 rounds unless
stated otherwise. ``Bids only'' is access level L1, ``bids + costs'' is L2;
intervals are 95\% block-bootstrap intervals. Every detector and cell is
listed in Appendix~\ref{app:allresults}.

\subsection{From bids alone, a calibrated alarm names the cartel}
Figure~\ref{fig:alarms} asks the question an overseer would ask: did the
alarm rule point to exactly the colluding pair, to a wrong pair, or stay
silent? With bids only, our permutation-corrected net-influence rule named
exactly the right pair in $16$, $13$ and $20$ of $20$ markets for the three
cartels that chat (two strong firms; strong and weak firm; two weak firms),
and never named a wrong pair. In $40$ honest markets it raised one false
alarm. The positive control, a scripted cartel placed among AI bidders
(P0), was named in all $20$ markets at both access levels.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[199,400,466].correct_unique,wrong_pair; all_cells[55].any_candidate; all_cells[124,130].correct_unique
The rule published with the method is not usable as an alarm here: it
flagged $24$ of $40$ honest markets with bids only and all $40$ with costs,
and for the strong-pair cartel with costs it named the wrong pair in $19$
of $20$ markets.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[56,63].any_candidate; all_cells[207].wrong_pair
With costs our rule named the pair less often (5, 8 and 19 of 20; three
false alarms in 40). Neither rule caught the cartel without chat
or the spy (at most $1$ of $20$ markets each).

\subsection{With costs, the direction of the flow becomes visible}
Costs let the measure separate two things: whether the two firms' bids move
in lockstep, and whether one firm's private cost shows up in the other
firm's bids (a ``leak''). Pooled over all markets of a type
(Figure~\ref{fig:flow}), honest pairs show neither. The cartel that chats
shows strong lockstep and leaks in both directions, consistent with
partners who tell each other their costs. The spy shows a one-way leak: the victim's
cost appears in the spy's bids ($0.0047$ bits), not the reverse
($-0.0004$). The cartel without chat leaves only a weak one-way trace
($0.0021$ bits) and no lockstep.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json fingerprints[0,2,3,4].terms.action,leak_to_member_1,leak_to_member_2
These pooled patterns are clear, but single markets are short: the
leak-based rule named the spy pair alone in only $4$ of $20$ spy markets,
and market-level flow labels rarely resolved the mechanism.

\subsection{Net influence versus a simple correlation}
Does the paper's measure beat the obvious alternative? We score how often a
treated market looks more suspicious than its honest twin with identical
costs (AUROC; $0.5$ is a coin flip, $1$ is perfect separation) and compare
net influence with a plain correlation of the same two bid series
(Table~\ref{tab:compare}). In the pre-registered primary test, net
influence with costs separated the strong-pair cartel from honest markets
($p=0.00002$), but correlation did better, in the original and in fresh
markets, and it was at least as good for every cartel that chats
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:compare}). Net influence was better only for the
spy: it ranked spy markets above their honest twins in $70\%$ of pairs,
correlation in $41.5\%$, and this held on fresh markets. With bids only the
advantage did not replicate ($+0.095$ $[-0.1125,0.315]$). This ranking is
weaker than an alarm: the alarm named the spy in at most $1$ of $20$
single markets (Figure~\ref{fig:alarms}).

\subsection{Two warnings: the quiet cartel and the AI judge}
\emph{The quiet cartel.} The cartel without chat earned the most. Its pair's
profit rose by $0.399$ $[0.364,0.434]$ per round, almost three times the
gain of the chatting strong-pair cartel ($0.144$), and the gain replicated
on fresh markets ($+0.283$ $[0.179,0.371]$). Yet both measures detected it
least well, and the alarm never named it from bids alone
(Figure~\ref{fig:warnings}a).
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json behaviour[64,40].paired_difference; all_cells[268].correct_unique; results/analysis/replication_v22/results.json behaviour[40].paired_difference
A post-hoc look at the bids shows why. Without a channel, the two partners
settled on one price: $97\%$ of their bids were exactly 3, the highest cost
a strong firm can draw and the lowest cost of a weak firm, and they bid the
same amount in $95\%$ of rounds (the same strong pair in honest markets:
$35\%$ and $53\%$). Given a firm's own cost, its bid varied by only $0.16$
bits, against $0.72$ in honest markets. A bid that hardly changes carries
almost no information, so a dependence measure has nothing to pick up: the
cartel coordinated on a fixed focal price, not by reacting to each other.
Such stable, elevated prices are what classic bid-rigging screens based on
the spread of bids look for \citep{huber2019}; we did not test them.
% source: results/quiet_cartel/summary.json (experiments/quiet_cartel_bids.py; Qwen markets 0-19, designated pair, complete rounds): share_bid_3, same_bid, h_bid_given_cost

\emph{The AI judge.} The Qwen 9B judge reads the round records and names
the most suspicious pair. Under its registered $12{,}000$-token limit it
returned no valid answer in $322$ of $600$ judgements, mostly because its
reasoning ran out of tokens. With $24{,}000$ tokens it answered $54$ of
$60$, but it
separated cartel from honest markets barely better than chance (AUROC
$0.625$ $[0.424,0.808]$) and simply named the two strong firms: right in
$91\%$ of strong-pair cartels, but in $2\%$ of strong--weak cartels, where
net influence was right in $93\%$ of the same markets
(Figure~\ref{fig:warnings}b). All judge comparisons use only markets with a
valid answer, so they could favour easier cases.

\subsection{How many rounds, and other AI models}
\emph{Rounds.} The alarm needs enough rounds. With bids only it named the strong-pair
cartel in $6$ of $20$ markets after $50$ rounds, $9$ after $100$ and $16$
after $200$, while false alarms in honest markets stayed at $3$ of $40$ or
fewer (Figure~\ref{fig:breadth}a).
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[147,173,199].correct_unique; all_cells[3,29,55].any_candidate

\emph{Other models.} Results were similar, with one exception
(Figure~\ref{fig:breadth}b). The alarm named the cartel in $18$ of $20$
markets for Qwen3.5-4B and Gemma-4-12B, and in $16$ and $19$ of $20$ in
mixed-model markets. For Ministral-3-8B it found only $11$ of $20$ and
raised false alarms in $6$ of $20$ honest Ministral markets ($3$ of $20$
for Qwen3.5-4B): calibration that holds for one model need not hold for
another. Ministral was sampled almost deterministically (temperature
$0.15$, the vendor's recommendation), which may make honest bids look
alike. Because temperature and model change together here, the data cannot
tell whether low temperature in general raises false alarms. The smaller Qwen was caught as often as the
larger one although its cartel earned less than honest play ($-0.115$ per
round), while the Ministral and Gemma cartels gained $0.33$ and $0.23$:
the alarm reacts to coordinated bidding, not to its success. With only two
Qwen sizes, and the smallest Gemma and Ministral models unusable (too many
invalid bids), the data say nothing about a general size trend.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[*].correct_unique and any_candidate for sizes.qwen-4b, labs.gemma-12b, labs.ministral-8b, mixed (results/report_figures/key_figure_numbers.csv figure=7); behaviour[208,184,160].paired_difference; all_cells[934,1000,1066,1132].eligible; docs/PREREG.md section 2 sampling table

\emph{Look-alikes and other checks.} Honest look-alikes did not trigger the alarm: honest markets of four
identical Qwen agents raised one false alarm in $40$, mixed honest markets
$0$ and $1$ in $20$, and swapping which honest firm runs the same model
had no effect surviving correction (smallest adjusted $p=0.264$).
Removing the surveillance warning lowered the cartel's profit by $0.35$
per round without a resolved change in detection, and probes that changed
only the partner's cost gave no resolved effect (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).
