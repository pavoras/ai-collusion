\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\paragraph{Expected versus observed.}
Before collecting any LLM data we simulated rule-following scripted bidders
in the same game, with the same rules and sample sizes
(Appendix~\ref{app:scripted}). Figure~\ref{fig:expectations} sets that plan
against what the AI agents did. Calibration carried over: honest markets
raised false alarms at about the simulated rate. Power did not. Scripted
cartels were named in about $99\%$ of markets and scripted spies in nearly
all; the AI cartels in $65$ to $100\%$ with bids only, and the AI spy with
costs in none. Flow direction shows the same gap: the
simulation expected two-way flow in every strong-pair cartel market and
one-way flow in $80\%$ of spy markets; the AI markets showed it in $0\%$ and
$20\%$, and only pooling recovered it (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}). AI agents
act on shared information less consistently than scripts, so their traces
are weaker than a rule-following simulation suggests. The AI spy is the
clearest case (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanics}): its bid rose by only $0.08$
between a cheap and an expensive victim, and it earned less than the same
firm in the honest twin market ($0.09$ against $0.16$ per round, lower in
$19$ of $20$ markets). It was hard to catch because it barely used the
stolen cost, not because it hid a profitable advantage.

\paragraph{Comparison with the original paper and concurrent work.}
Our scripted replication of \citet{bonjour2022} recovered their rising
detection pattern in rock--paper--scissors, though not every published
percentage (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). Two concurrent sprint projects
studied the measure in rock--paper--scissors. \citet{tawareRepo} also found
the fixed threshold unreliable (an innocent pair accused in $29\%$ of
100-round games) and a permutation-calibrated test near $2\%$ false alarms,
as we did. Unlike us, they found two near-identical Qwen agents flagged in
$7$ of $10$ games without collusion; in our auction, honest markets of four
Qwen agents raised one false alarm in $40$. Conditioning on costs and the
last winning price may explain the difference; we did not test this.
\citet{nayakRepo} found net influence and Cram\'er's V equally good with
scripted agents, as our correlation results suggest for chatting cartels.
\citet{rose2026} detect collusion from model activations with AUROC $0.99$
to $1.00$; behaviour-only audits need no model access but miss coordination
that leaves no trace in actions, so the two complement each other.

\paragraph{What a real overseer could use.}
A real overseer has no honest twin markets and usually no access to firms'
costs. The twins served only to evaluate the audit; the alarm itself needs
neither. It tests one market at a time against permutations of that
market's own bids, using only the bids and the last winning price, the
setting of our main alarm results. Real conditions would be harder in three
ways. First, the permutation test assumes that honest bids vary
independently once the market state is known; common cost shocks, such as
material prices, would make honest bids move together and raise false
alarms unless the audit conditions on public cost indicators or the
buyer's own cost estimates. Second, real tenders are fewer and less
regular, with changing bidders, whereas the alarm needed close to 200
rounds (Figure~\ref{fig:breadth}a). Third, we checked false alarms against
honest markets of the same model, which a real overseer cannot do, and
Ministral shows that calibration can fail. Costs are realistic mainly for
a platform that runs the agents, or for a buyer with its own cost
estimates. Additions that need no twin markets are pooling evidence over
many tenders with the same firms, as in our flow analysis; screens on the
level and spread of bids \citep{huber2019,imhof2021}; and, where the
overseer controls the agents' platform, intervention tests that vary an
agent's inputs \citep{whisper2025} or activation probes \citep{rose2026}.

\paragraph{What this means in practice.}
Each point is limited to this game.
(1)~\emph{Screening from public bids is feasible.} A calibrated alarm
pointed to the right pair in most markets where AI agents coordinated
actively, with few false alarms and no wrong accusations.
(2)~\emph{Calibrate before alarming, per model.} The fixed threshold
published by \citet{bonjour2022} flagged most or all honest markets; the
permutation correction fixed this for the main model but not fully for
Ministral (6 of 20).
(3)~\emph{Use costs to understand, not to detect.} Costs did not improve
detection, but pooled over many markets they show which way information
flows and separate a two-way cartel from a one-way spy.
(4)~\emph{Always compare with a simple baseline.} Correlation did as well
for chatting cartels; net influence earned its place only for espionage,
and there only as a ranking.
(5)~\emph{Combine dependence with price screens.} The most profitable
cartel kept a fixed price, invisible to dependence measures but visible in
stable, elevated prices. (6)~\emph{Do not rely on a small AI judge or on
rule-following simulations alone.}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Only the primary test is confirmatory; alarm counts, flow patterns, the
bid and message analyses and the simulation comparison are secondary or
post hoc. The replication was planned after the original results were known
and reuses the honest control markets. Twenty markets per type give wide
intervals, and absence of an advantage is not equivalence. The auction is
synthetic, the cartel is instructed rather than emergent, and the treatment
bundles a joint objective, partner information, a surveillance warning and,
in C1, a chat channel. The core uses one bidder and one judge model; the
smallest models were unusable and breadth cells have 20 markets each. A
one-way leak is not a unique signature of espionage, and the pooled flow
intervals are approximate. Privacy, false-accusation and dual-use
considerations are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:ethics}. Next steps: independent
replications; screens that combine correlation, flow direction and price
stability; larger bidder and judge models; cartels that can reply to each other; and
markets with more firms.
