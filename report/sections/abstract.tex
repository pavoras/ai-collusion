\begin{abstract}
Can an overseer find two colluding AI agents from their actions alone? We
test pairwise net influence, an information-theoretic dependence measure,
on language-model bidders in a repeated procurement auction with an
instructed two-firm cartel and matched honest markets, with and without
access to private costs. In the pre-registered primary test, net influence
separated cartel from honest markets (AUROC $0.865$, $p=0.00002$, $20$
matched blocks), but a plain correlation of the same data did better
($0.9975$), and correlation matched or beat net influence on every cartel
with a private channel.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json primary; all_cells[206,202].auroc
Net influence added information where one firm secretly saw a rival's cost:
it beat correlation by $+0.285$ AUROC, and by $+0.265$ $[0.070,0.445]$ on
fresh markets collected afterwards.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json contrasts[263].result; results/analysis/replication_v22/results.json contrasts rep.E1 "L2:net minus L2:correlation"
A cartel without a channel raised profit the most, also on fresh markets,
yet was the hardest cartel to detect. The published fixed alarm threshold
flagged all $40$ honest markets with cost access. An LLM judge with ample
token budget barely separated cartel from honest markets (AUROC $0.62$,
interval including $0.5$) and named the strongest firms rather than the
colluders.
% source: results/analysis/final_v1/results.json all_cells[63].any_candidate; results/analysis/judge24k_v23/results.json all_cells[138] judge:qwen-9b auroc
Behaviour-based screens can support an investigation, but should be
calibrated, compared with simple baselines, and not read as proof of
agreement.
\end{abstract}
