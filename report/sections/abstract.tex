\begin{abstract}
AI agents increasingly bid and set prices for competing firms. If two of
them secretly coordinate, can an overseer find the pair just by watching
what they do? We test a published information-theoretic measure, pairwise
net influence, on AI bidders in a repeated auction with an instructed
two-firm cartel and honest twin markets with identical costs. From bids
alone, a calibrated alarm built on the measure named exactly the colluding
pair in $49$ of $60$ markets where the cartel chatted in private, never
named a wrong pair, and raised one false alarm in $40$ honest markets; the
rule published with it flagged $24$ of $40$.

When the overseer also sees each firm's costs, the direction of information
flow becomes visible when pooled over markets: two-way for the cartel,
one-way from victim to spy in an espionage scenario. In single markets,
however, the alarm named the spy in at most $1$ of $20$. A plain correlation
of the same data detected chatting cartels at least as well (pre-registered
test: net influence AUROC $0.865$, $p=0.00002$; correlation $0.9975$); net
influence ranked spy markets above their honest twins more often, and
this replicated on fresh markets ($+0.265$ AUROC $[0.070,0.445]$).

Two warnings follow: the most profitable cartel, one without chat, kept a
fixed price and went almost unnoticed, and an AI judge named the strongest firms rather than the
colluders. Behaviour-based screens can direct an investigation, but need
calibration and a simple baseline, and do not prove that firms colluded.

\end{abstract}
