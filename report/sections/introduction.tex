\section{Introduction}
AI agents increasingly act for competing firms: they set prices, place bids
and negotiate. If two such agents coordinate in secret, an overseer may see
only what they do. Their messages may be hidden or encoded, and similar
bids alone prove nothing, because honest agents react to the same market.
Can an audit of behaviour still find the colluding pair?

\paragraph{Why this focus.}
Pairwise net influence \citep{bonjour2022} is a published candidate for
exactly this audit: it measures how strongly one agent's actions depend on
another's and subtracts the strongest dependence on any third agent, so that
a pair is flagged only if it moves together beyond what the rest of the
market explains. It needs only actions, not messages or model internals.
So far it has been tested with scripted agents in rock--paper--scissors,
where coordination is mechanical. Three questions decide whether it is
useful in practice, and they structure this paper. Does it find colluding
\emph{AI} agents in a market setting, and does its alarm hold up against
honest markets? Can it say \emph{what kind} of information flows, a mutual
cartel or one-sided espionage, if the auditor also sees private costs? And
does it beat a simple correlation and an AI judge that reads the same
records? We study a repeated procurement auction because bid rigging is a
classic cartel problem with clear private information (each firm's cost)
and a clear honest benchmark.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Four AI bidders compete for 200 rounds (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). In some
markets two bidders are told to form a cartel; in others one bidder
secretly sees a rival's cost. Every treated market has an honest twin with
identical costs. The auditor sees either the bids only or the bids and
every firm's private costs. It never reads messages.

Four findings stand out.
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{From bids alone, a calibrated alarm finds cartels that talk.}
It named exactly the cartel pair in most such markets, never a wrong pair,
and rarely raised false alarms (Figure~\ref{fig:alarms}). The uncalibrated
rule published with the method flagged most honest markets.
\item \textbf{With costs, one can see which way information flows.} Pooled
over markets, cartel partners leak their costs to each other, while a spy
receives information one way (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}). In single markets
this signal is too weak: the alarm almost never named the spy.
\item \textbf{A simple correlation detects talking cartels just as well.}
Net influence added value only where private information leaked: it
ranked spy markets above honest ones more often, also on fresh markets (Figure~\ref{fig:compare}).
\item \textbf{Two blind spots.} The most profitable cartel, one that never
talked, simply kept a fixed price and went almost unnoticed, and an AI judge named the strongest firms
instead of the colluders (Figure~\ref{fig:warnings}).
\end{enumerate}
Our simulation had predicted the false-alarm rate well but overestimated
how clearly AI agents reveal themselves (Figure~\ref{fig:expectations}).
The contribution is a pre-registered, matched-control test of what such an
audit can and cannot tell an overseer, with an out-of-sample replication of
the main secondary findings.
