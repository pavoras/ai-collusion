\section{Introduction}
Language-model agents increasingly act for competing firms: they set prices,
place bids and negotiate. If two such agents coordinate in secret, an
overseer may see only what they do. Their messages may be hidden or encoded,
and similar bids alone prove nothing, because honest agents react to the
same market history. Can a statistical audit of behaviour still find the
colluding pair?

We test one candidate audit, pairwise net influence \citep{bonjour2022}. It
measures how much one agent's action depends on another's and subtracts the
strongest dependence on any third agent; a pair that moves together beyond
what the rest of the market explains is flagged. We apply it to LLM bidders
in a repeated procurement auction with four firms, an instructed two-firm
cartel, and matched honest markets with identical costs. The auditor has
either public bids only (access level L1) or, in addition, every firm's
private costs (L2). With costs, the measure splits into action dependence
and directional ``leaks'' of one firm's cost into another firm's bid. The
auditor never reads messages; we do not test steganography.

Three findings stand out.
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Detection works, but a simple baseline does as well.} In the
pre-registered primary test, net influence separated cartel markets from
honest ones, but a plain correlation of the same data separated them even
better. On cartels that talk over a private channel, net influence showed
no advantage.
\item \textbf{The advantage appears when information leaks.} When one firm
secretly sees a rival's cost (espionage), net influence beat correlation at
L2, and this held on fresh markets collected after the main analysis.
\item \textbf{The most profitable collusion was the quietest.} A cartel
without a channel raised its joint profit the most of all LLM treatments,
again on fresh markets, yet it was the cartel that both detectors separated
least well from honest markets. The published fixed alarm
threshold flagged every honest market at L2, and an LLM judge, even with
ample tokens, named the strongest firms rather than the colluders.
\end{enumerate}
The contribution is a pre-registered, matched-control evaluation of what
such an audit can and cannot tell an overseer, including discrimination,
localisation of the pair, honest-market alarms, and an out-of-sample
replication of the secondary findings.
